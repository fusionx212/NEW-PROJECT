# Tonight's Sky, Softly. Boulder: Sunday 25 October 2026

Good evening, Boulder. This is Tonight's Sky, Softly. It is Sunday, the twenty-fifth of October. Make yourself cosy. The sky has been getting ready for you all day.

Let your shoulders drop. Breathe in, and breathe out. The Sun set this evening at five past six in the evening. The sky will be properly dark by twenty-five to eight in the evening. Today's daylight was seventeen minutes shorter than a week ago.

The Moon. Full Moon. Completely lit. A little way up in the east, in front of the stars of Aries. It's a full moon, rising as the Sun sets, and staying up all night long. The next full moon is in one night. In the old almanacs it's called the Hunter's Moon.

The planets. Saturn. Bright. A little way up in the east. Saturn is a planet so light for its size that, in a big enough bath, it would float. And for anyone awake before sunrise: Mars and Jupiter will be shining in the morning sky.

The stars. Vega. Very high in the west. It's a bright blue-white star that spins so fast it is squashed into an egg shape. The light you see tonight left it around two thousand and one, and has been travelling ever since. Altair. Very high in the south-west. It's a star that spins once every nine hours. The light you see tonight left it around two thousand and nine, and has been travelling ever since. Deneb. Almost straight overhead. It's one of the most distant stars you can see without a telescope. Its light has been travelling towards you for thousands of years.

Straight above your roof tonight is the constellation of Cygnus. Cygnus is the swan, flying down the Milky Way with its long neck stretched out. Its brightest stars make a shape called the Northern Cross.

And high up in the north-east is Cassiopeia. Cassiopeia is a queen on her throne. Her stars make a big letter W, or an M, depending on which way the sky has turned.

Coming up in the sky. The Orionids meteor shower is fading now, but still sending the odd shooting star. The shooting stars seem to fly out from Orion, and the best time to look is late at night, from somewhere dark.

Tonight's sky fact. The Moon is slowly drifting away from us, about four centimetres every year. That's about as fast as your fingernails grow.

Here's tonight's little mission. Look out of the window and try to find the Moon and Saturn, over in the east. If the clouds are in the way, that's all right. The sky is still there, shining above them. Here is tonight's sky once more, slowly. Moon, full moon, east. Saturn, east. Vega, west. Altair, south-west. Time to rest. Let the Moon and the stars keep watch for a while. Goodnight, and sweet dreams.
